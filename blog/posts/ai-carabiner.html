Can AI design a stronger carabiner?

A 3D-printed clip snapped, so I asked AI to make it stronger. It got the clip about five times stronger on paper, and taught me exactly where to stop trusting it.

I'd printed a carabiner-style clip in PETG at 100% infill to hold a canvas shade onto a metal hook, and it snapped at the bottom corner. Instead of opening CAD, I gave Claude the STL and a photo of the break and asked it to make the clip stronger. What came back was a series of redesigns, a homemade stress simulation and, best of all, a clear record of where an AI's engineering confidence runs out.

Safety first: this is a shade clip, not climbing gear. A home-designed or 3D-printed carabiner is unrated and untested and must never hold a person.

How it worked

Claude didn't use a CAD package. It sliced my STL in Python, rebuilt the clip's outline as a 2D shape, and then thickened, filleted and reshaped that outline in code before extruding it back into a printable, watertight mesh. To compare versions, it wrote a small finite-element stress simulation from scratch, pulling a hook against the top inside of the clip and the shade against the bottom.

Four versions

  • The original was 6 mm thick, with big 1.2 mm chamfers on the edges.
  • Reinforced v1 thickened the weak sections by about 1.25 mm and added a generous fillet in the bottom corner where mine had broken.
  • Max strength went to 9 mm thick, widened the back and bottom to about 9.5 mm and shrank the chamfers to 0.5 mm. All the extra width went on the outside so the hook still fits.
  • The D-shape rethought the shape. The clip was failing because the load runs through the middle of the opening while the solid back sits off to the side, so the back bends like a bow. Filling in the spine side made the back about 14 mm wide, and sloped seats now guide the hook and the shade ring into corners right beside it. Now the back gets pulled almost straight instead of bent. It ended up about 39 × 67 × 9 mm, nearly the same outside size.

Simulated breaking loads

For PETG at 100% infill, assuming the gate carries no load:

  • Original: about 11 kg
  • Reinforced v1: about 17 kg
  • Max strength: about 36 kg
  • D-shape, hook seated in its corner: about 60 kg (47 to 67 kg)

So, on paper, about five and a half times the original. Treating a third of the breaking load as safe for repeated wind gusts, that's about 20 kg per corner: plenty for a small or medium shade, though a big tensioned sail still wants a stainless hook.

Add the stress-map images from the chat if you saved them (the plots that coloured the clip by stress, with the weak point starred), plus a photo of the original break.

The limitations

This is the interesting part. Each of these came up during the project itself.

Its first estimates were wrong

Before running any simulation, Claude estimated the reinforced v1 would be about 2.2 times stronger and the max version about 5 times. Once the simulation ran, those came out at 1.6 and 3.3 times. It had strengthened the corner where my clip broke, but the highest stress was actually further up the back. To its credit it flagged the correction itself, but on "how strong is this?" a confident-sounding number from an AI is just a guess until something checks it.

The simulation is rough

The stress model was a simple 2D one written on the spot. By its own estimate it's only good to about ±30 to 50%, it assumes printed PETG breaks somewhere between 35 and 50 MPa, and it assumes the gate's latch takes no load. If the latch did share the load, the max version could be about three times stronger again, but you can't count on that once wind starts jiggling the clip.

It designed something I couldn't print

I printed the strongest version and the gate was fused shut. The latch gap had been carried over from the original at 0.2 mm, which is narrower than a 0.4 mm nozzle can reliably keep open, especially with PETG, which oozes. The original only printed separated because its big chamfers widened the gap at the top and bottom faces, and the redesign had shrunk those. Version 2 widened the gap to about 0.6 mm. It's the kind of thing anyone who prints a lot knows by instinct, and the model missed it until a real print failed.

The number that matters is untested

Every load above is simulated. None of these clips has been pull-tested.

Material and settings matter as much as shape

Some of the best advice wasn't about geometry at all. On a part this narrow, setting enough walls that the thin sections are all perimeter lines beats 100% infill. Nylon or carbon-fibre nylon bends instead of snapping, and polycarbonate is stronger again. ASA would handle UV better than PETG outdoors.

Verdict

As a design partner, AI was genuinely useful. It found the real cause of the failure (bending of an off-centre back), proposed a shape that fixes it, and turned that into a clean printable mesh in an afternoon. As an engineer, it needed checking at every step: its gut estimates were off, its simulation is approximate, and it didn't know a 0.2 mm gap won't survive a 0.4 mm nozzle until my printer showed it. The numbers are a starting point for a pull test, not a replacement for one.

Once you've printed v2 (the 0.6 mm gap version), add how it came out, and if you test it (hanging weights, a luggage scale), where and at what load it broke.
